# The Declaration: Stop Being a Wage Drudge

For all ordinary people who live for other people’s goals and wear themselves out in exhaustion and pain.

## Preface

### The Declaration: Stop Being a Wage Drudge — Welcome to the Era of Plain Living

The way of life passed down to us by the older generation is, at its core, a manifesto: work relentlessly, consume relentlessly, compete relentlessly, and spend your whole life toiling like a beast of burden to prove your worth. Overtime is a blessing, hardship is spiritual practice, keeping up appearances is a necessity, and since everyone else does it, so should you.

We declare this old manifesto obsolete.

The Declaration: Stop Being a Wage Drudge — Welcome to the Era of Plain Living. This is the core idea of this book, and also where it aims to lead you. The old drudge manifesto tells you to live for others’ targets: for your boss’s KPIs, for your relatives’ expectations, for the show you put on on social media. Plain living teaches you to live for yourself: go to work responsibly, sleep properly, reserve time for yourself, spend money only when you need to, and stay away from people and things that drain you.

Online, people keep telling you: you haven’t truly lived if you don’t buy a house, if you don’t get married, if you don’t check in at some internet-famous scenic spot. These words are also part of the drudge manifesto. They draw up a checklist for you and turn life into a list of tasks. You accept them all, giving up sleep, giving up weekends, giving up the things you truly want to do. Living by goals laid out by others, you grow weary, miserable, and unfree.

*That* is what it means to have wasted your life.

This book is not a guidebook, not a doctrine, not a creed. It is a seed, a small flame. It does not demand you follow it, nor judge you right or wrong. If one sentence strikes a chord in you, take it with you. If you disagree, just close it.

In recent years, more and more people have embraced plain living: no longer obsessed with crafting a polished public image, no longer chasing internet-famous check-ins, shopping for groceries and cooking seriously, keeping regular hours. Some call this lying flat. It is not. Lying flat means giving up. Plain living is choosing a different way of life: once you spent money to feel superior; now you spend money to feel secure. Once you lived for others’ eyes; now you live for your own sake.

It is not that we do not want to live well. We have lost ourselves. Work, overtime, working even on weekends, and whatever spare time we have left is spent forcing ourselves to study, work out, or run side hustles. We survive on takeout and drag ourselves through sleepless nights, all for “someday.” Someday when we are rich, someday when we are free — then we will start living.

That is not how life works. Life is not a stepping stone. Life itself is the destination. This shift is not about overthrowing anything. It is simply taking back, one by one, the life that was always yours.

# Reclaim the Right to Define Your Life

## 1. Who Is Living Your Life for You?

Who actually set most of the goals in your life?

Bucket lists are written by social media influencers. Milestones to hit before thirty are pushed by content creators. “You will be doomed if you skip the civil service exam” comes from relatives. “Buying a house is the only way to put down roots” is sales talk from real estate agents. Surrounded by these voices, you slowly mistake them for your own thoughts. After pouring every ounce of energy into checking them off, you look back and realize you have only finished assignments handed to you by strangers.

The “checklist life” is trending online: reducing the rich, messy experience of being alive into to-do items, as if you only pass the test once every box is ticked. Some people get up in the middle of the night to travel just to check in at a viral spot, queue for two hours to take the same photo everyone else takes, then spend thirty minutes editing it. Whether the place is actually fun or whether they are worn out becomes irrelevant. You are chasing a pre-packaged symbol, not a place you genuinely want to visit.

The trouble is these checklists contradict one another. You want eight hours of sleep, but the checklist says successful people sleep only four. You want rest on weekends, but the checklist says your peers are all grinding. You want a quiet meal, but the checklist says life needs ritual. Every demand sounds reasonable, and every one pushes you deeper into exhaustion. You obey all the rules, yet you never ask yourself: What do I actually want?

## 2. What Does It Mean to Truly Waste Your Life?

The worst trick of lines like “you will have wasted your life if you don’t visit this place” is how they quietly redefine what “wasting your life” means.

According to them, a wasted life means missing a destination, not owning a certain item, or failing to hit a benchmark. But look at people around you who are worn thin: they have houses, cars, respectable jobs, yet suffer from constant insomnia, find holidays more draining than work, and reply to work messages even while eating with family. Is that not a wasted life?

Truly wasting your life is not skipping a few tourist spots. It is living decades without a single day spent the way you like. You are exhausted yet keep scrolling on your phone; you need rest yet force yourself to hit the gym; you hate drinking yet smile through dinner parties; you only want one bottle of water but buy three just to qualify for a discount. Every “I don’t want to, but I have no choice” piles up, and becomes a whole lifetime.

So this book takes the opposite stance: skipping a place will not waste your life. Travelling somewhere only to sacrifice sleep, quality of life and enjoyment, draining yourself just to prove to others that you were there — *that* is wasting your life. Locations belong to other people; your days belong to you.

## 3. Two Ways of Living

In life, you choose between two paths.

The first treats life as an investment. Time, energy and health are chips, to be spent on things that bring returns: more money, greater options, freedom further down the line. This path is valid, and many people find fulfilment in it — but only if you know clearly what kind of freedom you seek and how many years you are willing to pay for it.

The second treats life simply as life. You go to work earnestly, take responsibility, earn to support your family, but do not stake all your time, sleep and mood on work and market ups and downs. Reserve a little space for yourself each day, so you can relax today, instead of waiting for some distant future to start living. This path is not inferior. It spreads happiness across every ordinary day, rather than promising it all sometime later.

The question is not which path is better. It is which one fits you. The trap is wanting the second life, while your body keeps running on the first — and ending up fulfilled by neither.

## 4. Responsibilities, Desires, and Borrowed Goals

Learn to tell three things apart in life.

Responsibilities must be borne. Going to work to earn money, caring for parents and children, repaying debts you owe — these cannot be avoided, and there is no need to run from them. Responsibility is the foundation of plain living.

Genuine desires may be satisfied. If you love running, run. If you crave a nice meal, eat it. If you save up for something you truly cherish, buy it. Time and money spent on your own authentic desires nourish you; they are not wasted.

Borrowed goals may be rejected. Others say you should be more ambitious, buy a bigger house, send your children to a better school. These are not your desires. They are other people’s expectations, trying to use your life to come true. Their defining feature is that they never end: once you hit one mark, another waits.

To tell which category something falls into, ask yourself three questions: Do I genuinely need this long-term, or am I afraid of being compared? How much sleep, time and peace of mind will it cost? Can I live with the worst outcome?

# Sleep Is Your First Line of Defence

If this shift can only start with one thing, let it be sleep.

Eight hours of sleep each day is a non-negotiable bottom line. Crunch deadlines, pressure from your boss, market swings — even if the company collapses tomorrow, they must not erode your sleep long-term. This does not mean you can never stay up late; emergencies happen. But late nights must not become routine, and you must not accept the bargain of trading sleep for performance.

Is sleep worth defending this fiercely? Yes. It is the foundation of everything. When you are sleep-deprived, everything annoys you, everything tires you, and your temper flares easily. The two hours you steal at night cost you tenfold in lost productivity the next day. The harm chronic sleep loss inflicts on your body cannot be offset by a number in your bank account.

For many people, work is not what steals sleep — their phones are. Lying in bed, worn out, you scroll short videos anyway. The more you scroll, the more anxious you grow; the more anxious you are, the harder it is to fall asleep. The next morning you stare at your dark circles and regret it. The last quiet moment of your day has been handed over to algorithms.

Guarding sleep is simple: set a fixed bedtime. When the time comes, put your phone out of reach. No work messages, no short videos, no mentally taxing tasks before bed. Read a light book, listen to soft music, or just lie there and let your mind wander. Your body needs time to wind down from excitement. Give it that transition.

Your employer may ask you to work overtime or stay on call, but they cannot demand your sleep. Sleep is your most basic promise to yourself. Once you give ground here, every other boundary will crumble.

# Reserve Time for Yourself Every Day

Once you sleep enough, the next step is to claim time that belongs only to you.

Set aside time each day that is fully yours. No work messages, no side-hustle plans, no forced self-improvement. Watch TV if you want, go for a run if you want, read or just stare into space. Not every minute needs to produce something. This time exists so you can be with yourself.

A note on exercise: do not push yourself to work out when you are already exhausted, just because someone online has a better physique. Exercise is good only when you feel like moving, not when you feel guilty for lacking discipline. Work is already draining. Resting is reasonable, and you do not owe anyone a workout every night. When fitness stops being an obligation and returns to being a pleasure, you are far more likely to stick with it.

The same applies to studying, reading and side hustles. They should not be the default for your free time. One of the most insidious phrases of our era is “use every spare minute to improve yourself.” It turns every fragment of time into a resource to exploit, and makes idling feel like a sin. But humans are not machines. Daydreaming, wandering, doing nothing — these are part of life, not waste.

You may say: I want personal time, but I do not get off work until nine. Precisely because your time is scarce, do not surrender it casually. When you get home, mute your phone, silence work groups, and tell yourself: from this moment, the evening is mine. The first few days will feel strange; you will worry you are missing something urgent. You are not. Truly critical matters will reach you by phone.

The point of private time is that it carves out a space in your life where work, other people and algorithms cannot reach. The bigger this space, the more your life feels like yours. If it shrinks to nothing, you are no different from a machine running nonstop.

# Rest Days Are for Resting

Keep two days every week entirely for yourself.

Definition of a rest day: stop working. No scheduled study, no weekly reviews, no side hustles, no workout check-ins. Hike if you like, camp if you like, or stay home and binge shows. This day exists for no output. It exists so you can feel human.

For many people, rest days are not rest — they are draining in a different way. Rushing from one social gathering to another, collapsing at night, and feeling even more worn out by Monday. Others cram a week’s self-improvement into two days: listening to English while hiking, filming content while camping. You cannot enjoy yourself fully, and you cannot study well, caught between two goals.

Do not reply to work messages or puzzle over work problems on rest days. Not every message is urgent. Urgent matters get phone calls. If you hold firm to your rest days, work cannot keep spilling into your life.

Many people cannot relax because guilt chases them: everyone else is grinding, how can I idle? But think about it: all that grinding does not guarantee freedom for those people. It may only trap them in more competition. You do not have to punish yourself with other people’s pace. Rest is not laziness. It recharges your life. When you are recharged, you have energy for work on Monday.

After a tough week, these two days refill you: physically, emotionally, mentally. If you never recharge, you will burn out sooner or later. When you break down, the company will hire a replacement. You are the one who pays the price.

# Spend According to Your Needs

The rule of consumption is simple: buy it if you need it; skip it if you do not.

No matter how viral an ice cream is, you do not have to spend forty yuan for one just to join the trend. If you only want one bottle of water, do not buy two for a discount. If your phone still works fine, do not upgrade just for new features. Expensive things are fine if you truly love them, will use them for a long time, and can afford them. Cheap things are still a waste if you have no use for them, even at one yuan.

Watch out for four consumption traps.

Chasing trends is the most common. When everyone posts about something, you feel outdated without it. But trends are manufactured by merchants and algorithms. Today it is fancy ice cream, tomorrow specialty coffee, the next week a famous photo spot. You can never catch up, and you only collect anxiety.

Overconsumption on credit is the second trap. Instalment plans and consumer loans are designed to let you spend future money for instant gratification. Every loan ties a debt to your coming days, and monthly payments eat away your future freedom. Paying interest for years for a possession is rarely worth it.

Stockpiling for discounts is the third trap. Promotions and buy-one-get-one offers look like bargains, but hoarding things you will never use is a pure loss. Your home fills with unopened goods and expired snacks, cluttering your space and your mind. Smart consumption means buying things you know you will finish.

Buying for status is the most hidden trap. Hosting lavish dinners, buying luxury goods to project an image, paying for check-ins — you are buying the version of yourself that others see, not the life you live. The hunger for superiority is bottomless; one purchase only makes you crave the next. Security is tangible: every extra bit of savings calms your mind.

Before buying, ask one question: Do I truly need this, or have I just been persuaded? This single question can cut half of your unnecessary spending.

# Step Away From Drains

The things that drain you are not only people around you, but also content on your phone.

First, people. There are those who habitually belittle you, pressure you, complain endlessly, and drag you into drama. They may mean no harm, yet after being with them, you feel irritable, fatigued and doubtful about yourself. You need to keep your distance. No arguments, no attempts to change them. You will not fix them; you will only lose your peace and sleep. Minimize contact, give minimal responses, refuse to absorb their negativity, and stay out of their mess.

Content drains you too. Low-quality short videos, anxiety bait, shopping hype — these all sap you. One video about body flaws makes you feel defective. A clip of a peer earning millions makes you feel like a failure. A product review makes you feel your life is lacking. You were calm before scrolling; afterward you are left thinking you are not enough. This is not entertainment. It is slow poisoning.

Algorithms excel at finding your softest spots and poking them over and over. Whatever makes you anxious gets pushed to you again and again, until you compare yourself against an imaginary race and run yourself ragged.

To tell whether something nourishes or drains you, check your state before and after. If you feel calm, happy or energized afterward, it nourishes you. If you feel irritable, inferior, empty or tempted to spend recklessly, it drains you. Keep the nourishing things; swipe away, block, unfollow the draining ones. Do not argue with videos, do not force yourself to watch. Attention is precious. Save it for people and things that feel good.

Pointless socializing is another drain. Dinners, drinking parties, obligatory networking — skip them if you do not want to go. You do not have to force it just to build connections. Relationships worth nurturing are the small number where you feel comfortable and supported, not thousands of casual contacts in your address book.

Stepping away from drains is not coldness. It is stopping losses. Your sleep, mood and time are limited. If you spend them on people and things that exhaust you, less remains for your own life.

# Work Is a Way to Make a Living, Not Your Entire Life

Work matters, but it must stay in its proper place.

At its core, work is trading your time for income to support your life. Do your job earnestly, honour your responsibilities, and do not shirk your duties — that is basic integrity. But keep this clear in your heart: work is only part of life, not the whole of it, and certainly not your purpose.

Many people turn work into everything: endless overtime for promotions, being on call at all hours to impress, pushing themselves to breaking point for fear of being laid off. Yet when layoffs come, the company will not spare you just because you worked the most overtime. When you fall ill, the project continues and someone replaces you. The health, family time and rest you sacrificed for work will not be compensated.

Occasional overtime is acceptable, but it must not become normal. Before agreeing to overtime, weigh the cost: Will it eat my sleep? Will it take my private time? Will it leave me broken tomorrow? If the price is too high, learn to say no. Refusing overtime is not irresponsibility; it is defending your life. Some overtime is worthwhile — a critical project phase, a chance to learn. Some is purely draining: performative busyness, all-nighters for trivial demands. Bear the worthwhile ones; turn down the draining ones firmly.

Job instability, layoffs and industry downturns are real risks. The right way to prepare is saving money for a buffer, not burning yourself out for a false sense of safety. If you ruin your health, your savings may only cover medical bills — the bitterest irony of all.

Remember: work sustains you; it should not mortgage your whole life. When the company needs you, you are a resource. When you need a life, the company will not look out for you. Earn what you need, guard your time, be with the people you love, and get your sleep.

# Stop Mental Churning

Mental churning is one of the quietest drains of modern life.

Much anxiety is not forced on you by circumstances — it is self-created by replaying thoughts. A clumsy remark from the day loops in your head at night. An unanswered message spawns ten imagined worst-case scenarios. Something already in the past plays on repeat, growing bigger and heavier until it crushes you.

Mental churning produces nothing. Every minute spent worrying cannot change the past or control the future. It only devours the present. When something happens, fix it if you can. If you cannot, accept it. Acceptance is not surrender. It saves your strength for what still matters.

Comparison is another source of churning. You see polished snapshots of other people’s lives: travel, promotions, new homes, fitness gains — and doubt yourself. But social media only shows edited highlights. No one posts their sleepless nights, anxiety or messy struggles. Racing against others’ highlight reels is a contest you can never win.

Mental churning and poor sleep feed each other. Sleep-deprived minds overthink; overthinking keeps you awake. The cycle locks you in. Break it by guarding sleep and your private time. When your days have structure and space to breathe, you have less room for spiralling thoughts.

The best fix is not simply “cheer up.” Build barriers: consistent sleep, protected personal hours, sacred rest days, and distance from draining people and content. A steady rhythm for life leaves fewer gaps for anxiety to creep in. Seek out things that soothe you. Keep away from people and things that hurt you. Good emotional health is not about suppressing feelings; it is about choosing and tending to your world.

## Epilogue

### A Seed

This book does not teach you to get rich quick, does not promise a dramatic comeback, and does not hand you a rigid checklist of rules. It only says one plain thing: sleep well, reserve time for yourself, truly rest on rest days, spend according to your needs, stay away from draining people and content, remember work is for a living not your whole life, and do not let mental churning steal your days.

These truths are not new. The hard part is knowing them yet daring not to slow down. You fear falling behind if you pause, fear being eliminated if you slack off, fear everyone else has handed in their paper while you remain stuck.

This book wants to say this for you: you may slow down. You do not need to live up to others’ expectations, complete their checklists, or drain every present moment for some distant “someday.” You work hard for happiness, not money as an end in itself. Happiness does not wait until all conditions are perfect. It lives in your sleep each night, in your private hours, in the sunlight on your rest days.

This book is a seed. It does not demand to sprout, nor guarantee harvest. It only reminds you quietly: your life is yours, and you hold the right to define it. If one day you mute work messages and set aside an hour just to daydream, or turn down a dinner you did not want to attend — the seed has already begun to grow.

The Declaration: Stop Being a Wage Drudge — Welcome to the Era of Plain Living. This is no empty slogan. It marks a shift for a generation: from living for others, to living for ourselves. The age of the drudge is over. From now on, life belongs to you.

Start with tonight’s sleep. Take your life back.
